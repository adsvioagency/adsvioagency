The 4-hour-a-month social media system

Not a growth-hacking scheme. A boring, repeatable system that produces twelve decent posts a month from one sitting, because consistency is what actually compounds.

By Pierre Runald Février Updated 30 August 2026 7 min read

The whole system in one line

One two-hour session a month to plan and write everything. Two hours across the month to shoot and publish. Five content types on rotation so you never face a blank page.

Why your last attempt stopped

Almost everyone has tried this. A few weeks of posting, very little back, a vague sense of foolishness, then it stops, followed by guilt about stopping, which is somehow worse than stopping.

It was not discipline. It was that you were deciding what to post on the day you posted it. That is the hardest possible version of the job: inventing strategy, writing copy and making a design in the twenty minutes before you open. Nobody sustains that.

Separating the deciding from the doing is the entire trick. Everything below is in service of that.

The monthly session: two hours, once

Put it in the calendar on the same day every month. Last Thursday, first Monday, it does not matter. Bring a coffee and your phone.

Part one: the review: 20 minutes

Open last month’s posts. Write down the three that got the most saves, shares or DMs. Ignore likes.

Saves and shares mean it was useful. Likes mean it scrolled past pleasantly. You are looking for a pattern in what people wanted to keep.

Part two: the plan: 40 minutes

Twelve slots for the month, three a week. Assign each one a content type from the five below. Do not write anything yet: just decide what each one is.

A rough rhythm that works: four Proof, three Teach, two Behind, two Answer, one Ask.

Part three: the writing: 60 minutes

Now write all twelve captions in one sitting. This is far faster than writing twelve captions on twelve different days, because you stay in the same voice and the same headspace.

Do not perfect them. A caption that took four minutes and went out beats a perfect one that never did.

The five content types

This is the part that removes the blank page. Every post you will ever make is one of these five.

1. Proof: you did the thing

Finished work. Before and after. A plate, a haircut, a completed job, a delivered project. This is your highest-converting type and the easiest to produce, because it is a by-product of working.

2. Teach: something you know that they do not

How to tell whether a colour will suit you. What to check before signing a lease. Why the cheap version fails after six months.

This is the type that earns saves, and saves are the metric worth watching. It also does the most for credibility, because knowing things is what people are actually buying.

3. Behind: how it gets made

The prep at 6am. The failed batch. The tool nobody else uses. This is boring to you and genuinely interesting to everyone else, which is the hardest thing to believe on this page.

4. Answer: a real question you get asked

Keep a note on your phone. Every time a customer asks something on the phone or at the counter, write it down. That note is a year of content and it costs nothing.

5. Ask: an actual invitation

Once a month, plainly: here is what I do, here is what it costs, here is how to book. One in twelve is the right ratio. People do not mind being sold to occasionally; they mind being sold to constantly.

The two hours across the month

The rest is shooting and publishing, and it happens in the gaps.

  • Shoot in batches. When you have a good result in front of you, take five photos, not one. Same for video.
  • Keep a folder. One album on your phone called Content. Everything goes in it as you go.
  • Schedule what you can. Meta’s own scheduler is free and adequate. Load the month in one go.
  • Reply within a day. This matters more than the posts. A comment answered two weeks later is a conversation that ended.
The rule that keeps it going

Never skip two in a row. One missed post is nothing. Two becomes a week, a week becomes a month, and a month is where people quit for good.

What to measure, and what to ignore

Watch thisBecause
Saves and sharesSomeone found it useful enough to keep or pass on
DMs and enquiriesThe only metric that turns into money
Profile visitsPeople moving from curious to interested
“I saw your post” in personUnmeasurable and the most reliable signal there is

Ignore follower count. Four hundred engaged local followers will out-earn forty thousand scattered ones, every time. Chasing the bigger number usually means posting things that travel widely and convert nothing.

Which platform

Two, properly, beats five badly. For most local businesses that is Instagram and Facebook. For consultants and B2B it is LinkedIn instead of Facebook.

Post the same core idea to both, adjusted for format. This is not lazy. Almost nobody follows you in two places, and the ones who do are not counting.

If you run it in more than one language

If your customers are split between languages, alternate rather than translating every post. Six in one, six in the other, with the important announcements in both.

Posting exclusively in the language that is easier for you is the most common version of this mistake, and it quietly tells half your audience the business is not for them.

A worked example month

Abstract advice is easy to agree with and hard to act on. Here is an actual twelve-post month for a salon, planned in one sitting.

Writing a caption that gets read

The first line is the whole game. Instagram truncates after roughly 125 characters, and that is the only part most people ever see.

  1. Line one: the hook. A specific claim, a question, or a number. Not “Happy Monday!”
  2. Lines two to four: the substance. Short sentences. One idea.
  3. Last line: what to do. Book, save this, ask a question, come in.

Write it the way you would say it to somebody standing in front of you. If you would not say the sentence out loud, do not post it.

On hashtags: three to five relevant ones, including your city. Thirty generic ones do nothing except make the caption look desperate.

Feed, stories and short video

These do different jobs and confusing them is why a lot of accounts feel like hard work.

FormatJobEffort
Feed postsYour permanent shop window. What a stranger judges you on.Planned
StoriesProof you are open and human. Disappears, so it can be rough.Casual
Short videoReach beyond your followers. The only format that finds new people at scale right now.Planned
HighlightsThe FAQ people actually read. Prices, hours, before-and-afters.Once, then rarely

If you only have capacity for one, make it feed posts. If you have capacity for two, add stories: they are the cheapest to produce and they do the most for the relationship with people who already follow you.

When to stop doing it yourself

There are two honest signals.

The first is that you have run the system for three months and it is working, but it is eating the hours you should be spending on the actual work. That is a good problem, and it is the point at which paying someone becomes an obvious trade.

The second is that you have tried three times and never got past week five. At that point the fourth attempt will not be different. Either hand it over or accept that social is not your channel and put the effort into your Google Business Profile and reviews instead, which for a local business is the better trade anyway.

Questions

Still wondering.

How long before this works?

Three months before it feels like it is doing anything, six before it reliably produces enquiries. That is not a sales tactic, it is just how long it takes an audience to form a habit around you. Most people quit at week five.

Do I need to be on video?

No, though it does outperform. Product, process, results and text-led posts carry plenty of businesses. If you are a coach or consultant, you are the product, and a face beats a logo, but a consistent faceless account beats an inconsistent one with your face on it.

What if I genuinely have no time at all?

Then do one post a week rather than three, and do not feel bad about it. Four consistent posts a month beats twelve for two months and then nothing. The system scales down without breaking.
