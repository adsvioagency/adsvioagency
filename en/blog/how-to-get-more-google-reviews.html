How to get more Google reviews without being annoying

Most businesses get reviews by accident, which means mostly from people who were annoyed. The happy customer who came back three times never leaves one, because nobody ever asked them.

By Pierre Runald Février Updated 30 August 2026 3 min read

Why asking feels awkward, and why it should not

Owners hesitate because it feels like begging. It is not. A customer who had a good experience is usually pleased to help — they just never think of it unprompted, because leaving a review is not on anybody’s mind at the moment they walk out happy.

You are not asking for a favour. You are removing the friction from something they would be willing to do anyway.

The four things that decide whether they do it

  1. When you ask. Immediately after the thing went well. Not a week later.
  2. Who asks. The person who did the work, not a system.
  3. How hard it is. One tap, or they will not.
  4. Whether you say what for. “Leave us a review” converts worse than “could you mention the colour match?”

The system, in five parts

1. Get your short link

Your Google Business Profile dashboard has a “Ask for reviews” option that gives you a short link going straight to the review box. Not to your profile — to the box. That difference alone roughly doubles completion.

2. Pick the moment

The best moment is the one where the customer visibly reacts well. The hair is finished and they are looking in the mirror. The job is done and they say thanks. That is the window, and it is about ninety seconds wide.

3. Use the channel you already use

In the Caribbean and across the diaspora, WhatsApp beats email for this by a wide margin. You are already in that thread with them. Email asks people to switch context, and most will not.

4. Say something specific

A script that works, adjusted to sound like you:

Really glad you liked it. If you have a minute, would you mind leaving a quick review? It genuinely helps people find us. [link]

Short, one link, no formatting. If you can add what to mention — the colour match, the turnaround, the fact you spoke their language — do, because a specific review is worth more than a generic one.

5. Put a QR code where people already stand

The counter, the receipt, the back of the card, the mirror. QR codes are free to generate and they catch the people you forgot to ask.

Four mistakes that cause real damage

  • Offering a discount for a review. Against Google’s policies and against FTC rules in the US. Reviews get wiped and profiles get penalised.
  • Buying reviews. Detected reliably now. The penalty is worse than the problem.
  • Only asking people you know were happy. Review gating — screening first and only sending the happy ones to Google — is explicitly against the policies.
  • Asking everyone at once. Forty reviews in one week after two years of silence looks exactly like what it is. Spread it out.

Replying is half the job

Reply to every review within a couple of days. On the good ones, be specific and brief. On the bad ones: stay calm, do not argue the facts, and offer to fix it offline.

You are not writing to the reviewer. You are writing to the next hundred people who read it, and they are judging how you handle a complaint far more than they are judging the complaint.

What to expect

A business seeing twenty customers a week that asks properly will usually add two to five reviews a month. That is enough to change how you look inside a quarter, and it keeps compounding because recency is part of what Google measures.

Questions

Still wondering.

How many reviews do I need?

Enough to beat the businesses you are competing with, which usually means checking the three above you on the map. Recency matters as much as volume — twelve from this year beats forty from 2021.

Should I reply to every single review?

Yes. It takes a minute, it is a visible signal that someone is paying attention, and it is one of the few things you fully control. A profile where the owner replies reads as a business that is still there.
