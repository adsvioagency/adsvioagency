What should I post on Instagram for my small business?

Start with a question someone asks before buying, booking or requesting an estimate. That gives the post a job beyond filling a square on the grid.

By Pierre Runald Février Updated 29 September 2026 3 min read

The short answer

Show real work with permission, explain a process, answer a common question or make a clear invitation. Choose examples that fit your service and audience. A manageable schedule and a working contact path matter more than copying a universal posting ratio.

Collect questions before you need a post

Keep a short list of questions from calls, messages and appointments. Remove personal details. Group them around service fit, preparation, price context and what happens next.

A consultant may explain the first session. A realtor may explain an approved listing process. A roofer may describe what information helps arrange an estimate. Use what the business actually does.

Five types with practical examples

  • Show: an approved finished project with a clear description.
  • Explain: why a step in your process matters.
  • Introduce: the person or team responsible for the work.
  • Answer: one common pre-purchase question.
  • Invite: who the service is for and how to inquire.

For an accountant, preparation for an appointment may be more useful than a generic motivational quote. For a salon, explain a service difference. For a healthcare or legal practice, use approved general information and protect private details.

Turn the ideas into a month

Choose a realistic number of posts and assign each one a purpose. An illustrative twelve-post mix is four work examples, three explanations, two process posts, two answers and one invitation. This is a planning example, not a formula proven to produce leads.

Use the monthly planning guide to organize drafting, assets, approvals and publishing. Reduce the volume if quality or accuracy starts slipping.

Replace filler with useful context

Hours and service updates can be useful when customers need them. The problem is a feed that never explains the work. Give a photograph context, describe the actual service and make sure any time-sensitive information is current.

Do not use a stock photograph as proof of your own project or invent a customer story to make a caption more persuasive.

Plan language and format deliberately

Write in a language your intended audience understands and that your team can support in replies. Translate important information carefully when needed. Do not assume alternating languages automatically serves every reader.

A photograph, carousel or short video can each work for a different explanation. Choose the format that makes the point clear and is realistic to produce.

Measure whether the post helps the next step

Look at relevant messages, profile visits and visits to the linked service page alongside reach, saves and shares. Check whether the people inquiring fit the service. Likes alone cannot establish business value.

Questions

Still wondering.

How often should a small business post?

Choose a schedule you can sustain with useful, accurate material. Start modestly and review the response before increasing volume. There is no universal posting frequency that guarantees inquiries.

Do I have to make Reels?

No. Use video when it helps explain or demonstrate the work. Clear photographs and written explanations can also be useful; review your own audience response.

What if I have no client photos to share?

Use general process explanations, service questions and accurate team introductions. Do not fabricate work examples or disclose private information to fill a content gap.
