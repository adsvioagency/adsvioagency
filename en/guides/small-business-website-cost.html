What a small business website should cost in 2026

The same three-page brief will get you a $500 quote and a $12,000 quote in the same week. Neither number is a lie. This explains what actually sits behind them.

By Pierre Runald Février Updated 30 August 2026 8 min read

The short answer

For a small business with four or five pages: $800 to $3,000 buys a properly built site from someone competent. Below $500 you are buying a template someone filled in. Above $8,000 you are paying for a team you probably do not need yet.

Why the same brief gets wildly different quotes

Because “a website” is not one thing. It is at least five separate jobs, and different providers include a different subset while using the same word.

Those jobs are: working out what the site should say, writing it, designing it, building it, and connecting it to everything else. A $500 quote almost always covers the fourth one only, and assumes you will do the rest. A $12,000 quote covers all five with a team of specialists on each.

Neither is dishonest. But if you compare them as though they are the same product, you will pick the cheap one, do four of the five jobs badly yourself, and conclude that websites do not work.

What you are actually paying for

Here is the honest breakdown of where the money goes on a small business site, and what each piece costs when bought separately.

The jobWhat it involvesBought alone
StrategyWho buys from you, what they need to see, what competitors show them, what the site has to say$300–$800
CopywritingEvery page written by someone who can write, in your voice, for a specific reader$500–$1,500
DesignLayout, type, colour, and how it behaves on a phone$500–$2,500
BuildTurning the design into a working, fast, accessible site$500–$3,000
Connect & launchGoogle Business Profile, analytics, booking, redirects, testing$200–$600

Add those up and the honest range for a competent small business site is roughly $2,000 to $8,000 at separate rates. A fixed-price package beats that because the same person is doing all five jobs in sequence, without a handover between each one.

The five routes, honestly compared

1. Do it yourself on a builder — $0 to $400 a year

Squarespace, Wix, Shopify. The tools are genuinely good now, and a determined owner can get something decent out of them.

The catch is not the tool, it is the four jobs it does not do. It will not tell you what to say, write it, or know that your Google Business Profile categories are wrong. Budget 20 to 40 hours of your own time, and be honest with yourself about whether you have them.

2. A marketplace freelancer — $150 to $800

Fiverr, Upwork at the low end. You will get a site. It will usually be a template with your logo and your text pasted in, delivered fast.

This works if you already have your copy written and know exactly what you want. It goes badly when you do not, because the freelancer has no incentive to tell you the brief is wrong — they are paid to deliver what you asked for.

3. A local freelancer — $800 to $3,000

Often the best value available, when you find a good one. You get a real conversation, someone who can push back, and usually decent copy.

The risks are capacity and continuity. One person gets busy, gets sick, or takes a full-time job. Ask directly what happens if they become unavailable halfway through.

4. A small agency or studio — $2,000 to $10,000

A team, a process, and someone accountable when a person is unavailable. At the lower end you get a productised package; at the upper end, custom work.

Worth it when you need more than a website — profiles, content, search setup — and want one party responsible for all of it. Less worth it if you genuinely only need four pages.

5. A full agency — $10,000 and up

Strategists, designers, developers, project managers. Excellent, and almost always the wrong purchase for a business under about $500,000 in revenue.

You are paying for capacity to handle complexity you do not have yet. Come back when you do.

The costs nobody puts in the quote

This is where budgets break. Ask about every one of these before you sign anything.

  • Domain — $12 to $25 a year. Make sure it is registered in your name, not the agency’s.
  • Hosting — $0 to $30 a month. A static site can genuinely be free. A heavy platform site is not.
  • Photography — $0 to $1,500. Stock is free-ish and looks it. Real photos of your actual business are the single biggest visual upgrade available.
  • Copywriting, if not included — $500 to $1,500, or many hours of your own time.
  • Revisions beyond the included rounds — often $75 to $150 an hour, and this is where a cheap quote quietly becomes an expensive one.
  • Maintenance — $0 to $200 a month depending on how it was built.
The question that saves the most money

“What is the total I will have paid you twelve months from today, assuming nothing goes wrong?” Anyone who cannot answer that in one sentence has not thought about your budget.

What a fair price looks like

For a small business with four or five pages, in 2026, from someone competent who includes strategy and copy:

  • $800 to $1,500 in markets with lower costs — the Caribbean, much of Latin America.
  • $1,800 to $3,500 in the US and Canada.
  • Add $1,000 to $2,500 if you also want profiles, starter content and search setup done properly rather than left to you.

Below those bands, something is being left out, and it is nearly always strategy and copy — the two that decide whether the site works. Above them, you are paying for capacity rather than quality.

Seven questions to ask before you sign

  1. What is the total, including everything, twelve months from now?
  2. Who writes the copy — you, or me?
  3. How many revision rounds are included, and what happens after that?
  4. Whose name is the domain registered in?
  5. Do I get the logins and the source files on launch day?
  6. What happens if you become unavailable halfway through?
  7. What is the deadline, and what happens if it is missed?

The last one is the most revealing. Most providers will not give you a date. The answer tells you more about how the project will go than any portfolio.

How to read a quote

Quotes for the same brief look different because they are describing different work. Three things tell you most of what you need to know.

Look for the word “copy”

If writing is not mentioned anywhere, you are writing it. That is the single most common hidden cost, and it is the reason so many small business sites end up with three paragraphs of placeholder text that never got replaced.

Ask directly: “Who writes the words?” The answer changes the price by several hundred dollars either way, and it changes whether the site works at all.

Look for a number of revision rounds

“Unlimited revisions” is usually a warning rather than a benefit. It tends to mean the provider has no process, expects churn, and will get slower as the project drags. Two rounds on an agreed direction is healthy.

Look for what happens at the end

Who owns the domain. Who holds the hosting account. Whether you get the source files. These are worth more than any design decision, because they determine whether you can ever leave.

What moves the price up

If you are being quoted above the bands earlier in this guide, one of these is usually why — and some of them are legitimate.

FactorWhat it addsTypical
Online payments or a shopProducts, checkout, tax, shipping rules, testing+$800–$4,000
A second languageTranslation as persuasion, plus hreflang and duplicate structure+$400–$1,200
Booking with real availabilityCalendar sync, staff, resources, deposits+$300–$1,500
Custom design rather than a systemMore rounds, more decisions, more build time+$1,000–$4,000
Migrating an existing siteAuditing what exists, redirects, keeping rankings+$200–$900
PhotographyShoot, direction, editing+$400–$1,500

Note what is not on that list: number of pages, past about page eight. Pages are cheap once the system exists. If someone is charging you meaningfully per page on a small site, ask why.

What moves the price down

  • Having your content ready. Photos, service list, prices, hours. A provider waiting three weeks for your logo is a provider billing for the wait.
  • Deciding fast. Most overruns are approval delays, not build delays.
  • Taking a package rather than a custom scope. A productised offer is cheaper because the process is already built.
  • Buying the whole thing at once. Website, profile and content together costs less than three separate engagements, because most of the thinking is shared.

A worked example

A salon in Miami wants a website. Here is roughly what the same job looks like across three routes.

RouteWhat actually happensYear one
Squarespace, DIYTemplate picked in an evening. Copy written by the owner over six weeks. Profile never touched.~$300
Marketplace freelancerTemplate filled with owner-supplied text in five days. Looks fine. No strategy, no profile, no content.~$600
Fixed-price packageStrategy, copy, four pages, profile claimed and categorized, social rebuilt, a month of content, review system.~$1,800

The first two are cheaper and both leave the highest-return work — the free work — undone. That is the actual difference, and it is not a design difference.

Questions

Still wondering.

Is a cheap website worse than no website?

Sometimes, yes. A slow site with template copy and no clear next step can be worse than a well-filled-in Google Business Profile and an active Instagram, because it looks like a business that stopped trying. If your budget is genuinely under $500, spend it on the profile and photography instead and come back to the website later.

How long should a small business website take?

Two to six weeks for four or five pages, once the provider has what they need from you. Longer than that usually means it is queued behind other work rather than being built slowly.

Should I pay monthly or once?

Once, for the build. Monthly is right for things that genuinely happen every month, like content or ads. Be careful with a monthly fee that covers the website itself — you often stop owning it the day you stop paying.
