The complete Google Business Profile guide for local businesses

This is the highest-return hour of work available to a local business anywhere in marketing, and it is free. Here is every field, what it does, and the order to do it in.

By Pierre Runald Février Updated 30 August 2026 8 min read

If you only do three things

Fix your categories, fill in every service with a description, and set up a way to ask happy customers for reviews. Those three move more than everything else on this page combined.

Why this matters more than your website

When someone searches “hair salon near me” or “Haitian food Miami”, the first thing on screen is not a website. It is a map with three businesses on it, each showing a rating, a photo, hours and a Call button.

Most people choose from that map without visiting anyone’s website. For a local business the profile is not a supporting act. It is the storefront. And because it is free, it is chronically neglected: plenty of businesses that spent $2,000 on a website have a profile with no categories and last year’s hours.

How Google decides who ranks

Google is unusually open about this. Local results are ranked on three things:

  • Relevance: how well your profile matches what was searched. Categories and services drive this.
  • Distance: how far you are from the searcher. You cannot change this, and it is why your competitor beats you for some searches and not others.
  • Prominence: how well known you are. Reviews, completeness, activity, and mentions elsewhere on the web.

Two of the three are within your control. Anyone selling you a guaranteed number-one ranking is ignoring the middle one.

Step one: claim and verify

Search your business name on Google Maps. One of three things is true.

  1. No profile exists. Create one at google.com/business. Easiest case.
  2. A profile exists, unclaimed. Click “Claim this business”. Google created it from other data.
  3. Someone else claimed it. Request ownership. It is often a former employee or an agency you stopped using. This takes a few weeks and is worth pushing through.

Verification is by postcard, phone, email or video depending on your category and country. Video verification is now common: you record a continuous clip showing your signage, your equipment and proof you have access to the premises. Do it in one take, in daylight.

Also search for duplicates. A business that moved, changed name or was listed by a supplier often has two profiles competing with each other. Report the extra one as a duplicate.

Step two: categories, which most people get wrong

Your primary category carries more weight than almost any other single setting. It should be the most specific accurate description of your main business, not the broadest.

“Restaurant” is worse than “Haitian restaurant”. “Hair salon” is worse than “Hair extension technician” if that is what you mostly do. Specific beats broad, because specific is what people actually type.

Then add secondary categories for everything else you genuinely do. A salon that also does nails and lashes needs all three, or it is invisible for two-thirds of its own services.

How to find the right categories

Look at the three competitors currently ranking above you for your main search. Their categories are visible in the page source, and there are free tools that read them for you. Copy what works, then add what makes you different.

Step three: fill in everything

Google measures profile completeness and rewards it. Most competitors stop after the name and phone number, which is your opening.

  • Name: your real business name exactly. Adding keywords is against the guidelines and gets profiles suspended.
  • Address: formatted exactly as it appears on your website and everywhere else. Character for character.
  • Phone: a local number if you have one. It outperforms toll-free.
  • Hours: including holiday hours. Wrong hours generate one-star reviews from people who turned up to a closed door.
  • Website: link to the most relevant page, not always the homepage.
  • Description: 750 characters, written for a person. Say what you do, who for, and what makes you different.
  • Opening date: a small trust signal, and free.
  • Attributes: wheelchair access, outdoor seating, women-owned, languages spoken. These appear as filters people search by.

Step four: services and products

List every service as a separate entry with its own description. This is one of the most underused parts of the whole profile, and it feeds directly into relevance.

Add prices where you reasonably can. Published prices filter out the wrong enquiries before they reach you, which is worth more than the enquiries you lose.

Step five: photos

Profiles with recent photos get meaningfully more clicks and direction requests. What matters is recency and authenticity, not production value.

  • Logo and cover: the two Google shows most often.
  • Interior: so people recognise the place when they arrive.
  • Exterior: from the direction people approach from. This genuinely helps them find you.
  • The work itself: food, hair, the finished job.
  • The team: faces build more trust than rooms.

Ten good photos beat forty bad ones. Add two or three a month rather than forty once. Recency is part of what is being measured.

Step six: reviews, which are the whole ballgame

Four things count: how many, how recent, the average, and whether you reply.

Most businesses have no system for asking, so reviews arrive by accident, disproportionately from people who were annoyed. Building a system is the highest-return thing on this page.

The system is not complicated:

  1. Get your short review link from the profile dashboard.
  2. Decide the moment to ask: right after the thing went well, not a week later.
  3. Ask in the channel you already use with that customer. WhatsApp works far better than email in most of our markets.
  4. Put a QR code on the counter and on receipts.
  5. Reply to every review, good and bad, within a couple of days.

On bad reviews: reply calmly, do not argue, and offer to fix it offline. You are not writing to the reviewer. You are writing to the next hundred people who will read it.

Never buy reviews and never offer a discount for one. Google detects it, and the penalty is worse than the problem you were solving. There is a fuller walkthrough of the asking system in how to get more Google reviews without being annoying.

Step seven: keep it alive

Dormant profiles slide. Twenty minutes a month covers it: post something, add a photo or two, answer new questions in the Q&A section, and check the hours are still right.

The Q&A section is worth a special mention because almost nobody uses it. You are allowed to post questions and answer them yourself. Put the five things people always ask on the phone there.

Posts, offers and events

Google Posts appear on your profile and expire after a week unless they are events or offers. Almost nobody uses them, which makes them cheap attention.

  • Update: anything current. A new service, a seasonal dish, a photo of finished work.
  • Offer: has a start and end date and shows a small banner. Good for genuine promotions, bad if it is permanently running.
  • Event: stays visible until the date passes. Useful even for small things: a late opening, a guest stylist, a workshop.

One a week is plenty. The value is less about the individual post and more about the profile visibly having a pulse.

Messaging and booking

Turn messaging on only if you will actually answer it. Google shows a response-time indicator, and a slow one is worse than no messaging button at all.

Booking is more interesting. If your scheduling tool integrates, appointments can be made directly from the map result without the customer ever reaching your website. For a salon or a clinic this is often the highest-converting path you have, and it bypasses every other decision on this page.

The performance data worth looking at

The profile dashboard shows more than most owners realise. Three numbers are worth a monthly glance.

What it showsWhat to do about it
Searches people used to find youAdd the recurring ones to your services and description, in the exact words they used
Calls, direction requests, website clicksIf views are high but actions are low, the photos or the reviews are letting you down
Discovery versus direct searchesDirect means people already knew your name. Discovery is the number you are trying to grow

The searches report is the most useful and the most ignored. It tells you, for free, the exact language your customers use, which is the thing every copywriter is guessing at. It is also where most of the useful work in local SEO actually starts.

If your profile gets suspended

It happens, and it is usually fixable. The common causes are keywords stuffed into the business name, a virtual office or mailbox used as a physical address, a category that does not match the actual business, or too many edits in a short window.

  1. Fix the underlying problem first. Appealing without fixing it fails.
  2. Gather evidence: signage, a utility bill, a business licence, photos of the premises.
  3. Submit the reinstatement form once. Submitting repeatedly slows it down.
  4. Expect two to four weeks.

A monthly routine that keeps it healthy

Twenty minutes, once a month, covers everything this guide describes after the initial setup.

  1. Add two or three photos.
  2. Publish one post.
  3. Reply to every new review.
  4. Answer any new questions in the Q&A section, and add one yourself.
  5. Check the hours, especially before a holiday.
  6. Glance at the searches report and note anything new.

That is the entire ongoing commitment. It is less work than a single social post, and for a local business it returns considerably more. If you would rather not do it at all, we set it up and hand it over.

Questions

Still wondering.

Do I need a physical address?

No. You can run a service-area business, define the areas you cover and hide the street address. That is the correct setup for consultants, trades and anyone working from home. Verification is sometimes fussier.

Can I put keywords in my business name?

No. It is against Google's guidelines and it is one of the most commonly reported violations, usually by your competitors. Profiles get suspended for it and getting reinstated is slow.

How do I get a review removed?

You can report a review that breaks the policies: spam, hate, a conflict of interest, or one that is clearly about a different business. You cannot get a review removed for being negative, and trying to is a poor use of your time. Reply well instead.

Is Google Business Profile the same as Google My Business?

Yes. Google renamed it in 2021 and retired the standalone app. Management now happens in Search and Maps directly, which is why old tutorials do not match what you see.
