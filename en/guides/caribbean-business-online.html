Pillar guide

The Caribbean and diaspora business owner’s guide to getting found online

Everything in one place, in the order that produces the most for the least. Written for businesses whose customers do not all search in the same language.

By Pierre Runald Février Updated 30 August 2026 7 min read

If you read nothing else

Do these in order: Google Business Profile, a system for asking for reviews, one social platform done consistently, then a website you own. The first two are free and beat the fourth for a local business.

The order that actually works

Most businesses spend their first marketing money on a website and never touch the free things that would have moved more. Here is the sequence, with what each step costs.

StepWhat it doesCost
1. Google Business ProfilePuts you on the map for everyone searching nearbyFree
2. A review systemTurns happy customers into public proof, on purposeFree
3. One social platformGets you seen by people who were not lookingFree + time
4. A website you ownHolds prices, policies and the decision$800+
5. Content, consistentlyCompounds all four aboveTime or $250/mo

Everything below expands on those five, and each has a full guide behind it.

Step one: the map listing

For a local business this is the front door, not the website. Someone searches “barber near me”, gets a map with three businesses, and picks from it, usually without visiting anyone’s site.

Categories matter most, completeness second, photos third. Getting the categories right is the single biggest free change available to most businesses.

The complete Google Business Profile guide covers every field, in order, including the awkward cases where somebody else claimed your listing years ago.

Step two: reviews, on purpose

Most businesses get reviews by accident, which means mostly from people who were annoyed. The customer who came back three times never thinks to leave one, because nobody asked.

A system is: your review link, a decision about when to ask, the channel you already use with that customer, and a QR code on the counter. In our markets WhatsApp outperforms email for this by a wide margin.

Step three: pick one platform and be consistent

Two platforms done properly beats five done badly, and one done consistently beats two done erratically.

The hard part is never the posting, it is deciding what to post. The 4-hour-a-month system separates the deciding from the doing, which is the only thing that makes it survivable alongside running a business.

Step four: a website you own

The site is where someone decides. It holds the prices, the service list, the policies and the proof: the things a feed cannot hold and a map listing has no room for.

It is also the only asset here that cannot be taken away by a platform. A hacked account or a policy strike ends an Instagram-only business overnight.

On whether you need one at all, the honest version is in do you need a website if you have Instagram?, including the cases where the answer is no. On what it should cost, what a small business website should cost in 2026.

What is different for Caribbean and diaspora businesses

Everything above applies to any small business anywhere. These four things are specific to ours, and they are where most generic advice stops being useful.

Your customers search in more than one language

A Haitian restaurant in Miami has customers typing “Haitian food near me” in English and customers typing in Kreyòl. A salon in Santo Domingo has both Spanish and English searches. These are different queries and they return different results.

Which means your profile description, your services and your site copy should contain both. Not a translated duplicate of the page, but the actual words people type, in the places that get read.

Picking one language quietly costs you half the audience

English-only tells first-generation customers the place is not really for them. Kreyòl-only or Spanish-only loses the second generation and the wider neighbourhood.

The answer is not to pick. It is to decide which language leads and which supports, based on who actually walks in, and then be deliberate about it everywhere.

WhatsApp is the contact channel, not the form

Across the Caribbean and the diaspora, WhatsApp is where people actually make contact. A contact form that emails you is a channel most of your customers will not use.

Click-to-chat on every page, on the map listing, and on a QR code by the register. It converts better than a form and it is free.

Word of mouth is strong, and it has a ceiling

Bouch-a-zòrèy works. Church on Sunday, the barber, the family WhatsApp group. Plenty of businesses have been built entirely on it.

The ceiling is that it only travels as far as your existing customers’ circles. The neighbour who is not from your community, the person who moved in last year, the second-generation customer who searches in English: none of them are in that circle. Everything in this guide is about reaching them without losing the circle you already have.

What this costs, realistically

Steps one to three are free and cost you time. Step four is where money starts, and the honest range for a competent four or five page site is $800 to $1,500 in the Caribbean, $1,800 to $3,500 in the US and Canada. That is broken down properly in the cost guide.

If your total budget is under $500, do not spend it on a website. Spend it on photography and do steps one to three yourself. You will be further ahead.

A 90-day plan

If you want a schedule rather than a list, this is the order we would run it in, and it assumes you are doing it yourself around a working week.

Month one: the free things

  1. Claim and verify your Google Business Profile.
  2. Fix the categories. Specific primary, secondaries for everything else you do.
  3. Fill in every field: services with descriptions, hours, attributes, a real description.
  4. Add ten good photos, including the exterior from the direction people approach.
  5. Set up the review link, a QR code and a habit of asking.

Cost: nothing but time. This month alone puts most businesses ahead of their nearest competitors, because most competitors have not done it.

Month two: the presence

  1. Pick one social platform. The one your customers use, not the one you like.
  2. Rebuild the profile: bio, link, highlights, contact button.
  3. Run one month of the 4-hour system. Twelve posts, planned in one sitting.
  4. Keep asking for reviews. This does not stop.

Month three: the thing you own

  1. Four pages: home, services, about, contact.
  2. Prices published, or at least a range.
  3. WhatsApp click-to-chat on every page.
  4. Point the profile and the social bio at it.

By the end of ninety days you are findable by people who have never heard of you, you have public proof, you have somewhere durable to send people, and none of it depends on a platform that could suspend you.

If you have no budget at all

Everything in month one and month two is free. That is not a consolation prize: for a local business it is the majority of the available return.

The honest ranking of free things by what they return:

  1. Google Business Profile categories and completeness.
  2. Asking for reviews, consistently.
  3. Ten decent photos taken on your phone in daylight.
  4. Answering every message and review within a day.
  5. Posting twice a week to one platform.

If you do those five and nothing else, you will be ahead of most businesses in your category who have spent money. Come back for the website when there is budget.

The mistakes we see most

  • Building the website first. The most common and the most expensive. The free work returns more and gets skipped.
  • Publishing no prices. It does not create enquiries, it creates the wrong enquiries and wastes your week.
  • A contact form as the only channel. In our markets that loses most of the audience. WhatsApp is where people are.
  • Letting the profile go stale. Last year’s hours generate one-star reviews from people who arrived at a closed door.
  • Posting only in the easier language. Usually the owner’s preference rather than the customers’.
  • Quitting social at week five. Three months is when it starts working. Almost everyone stops before that.

How to know it is working

Not traffic. These are the signals that actually mean something for a small business.

SignalWhat it means
“I found you on Google”The profile work is landing. Ask every new customer how they found you and write it down.
Reviews arriving without promptingThe habit has taken. This is the one that compounds.
People arriving already informedYour published prices and service list are doing the qualifying for you.
“I saw your post”Unmeasurable, and the most reliable indicator there is.
Fewer wrong enquiriesOften the first thing to change, and the easiest to miss.

Questions

Still wondering.

Where should I start if I have nothing at all?

Google Business Profile, today. It is free, it takes an afternoon, and for a local business it moves more than anything else on the list. Then set up a way to ask for reviews. Only then think about a website.

Do I need to be in every language my customers speak?

In your profile description and service list, yes, because that is where the searches happen and it costs nothing. On the website, one language leading with the other present is usually enough. Full duplication is a maintenance burden most small businesses should not take on.

Is any of this different if my business is in Haiti rather than the diaspora?

The mechanics are identical. What changes is connectivity, payment processing and how much of your audience is on mobile data, which pushes even harder toward a fast, light website and WhatsApp as the contact channel.
